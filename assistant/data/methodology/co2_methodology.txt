CO2 EMISSIONS PER CAPITA - METHODOLOGY

Definition
CO2 emissions per capita measures the tonnes of carbon dioxide emitted per person in a country in a given year. It includes emissions from burning fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) and from cement production, but excludes land-use change emissions.

Data source
The data comes from the Global Carbon Project (GCP), published and maintained by Our World in Data. The GCP is a joint initiative of the Scientific Committee on Problems of the Environment (SCOPE) and the International Geosphere-Biosphere Programme (IGBP). National emissions estimates are compiled from energy statistics published by the International Energy Agency (IEA) and the US Energy Information Administration (EIA).

How the risk score is calculated
Raw values represent tonnes of CO2 per person per year. To produce a comparable risk score, values are normalized to a 0-10 scale using min-max normalization across all countries in the dataset. A score of 0 means the lowest emitter in the dataset; a score of 10 means the highest emitter. High CO2 emissions per capita indicate greater climate risk and potential future carbon pricing exposure for supply chains sourced from that country.

Interpretation in ESG context
For supply chain risk purposes, high CO2 emissions indicate that a country's economy is heavily reliant on fossil fuels, which exposes suppliers to transition risk (carbon taxes, regulatory changes) and physical climate risk. Countries with scores above 7 typically include major oil producers or economies with energy-intensive manufacturing. Countries with scores below 3 are generally low-income countries or those with high renewable energy penetration.

Limitations
Per-capita figures do not capture total national emissions, so large low-income countries may appear relatively clean on a per-capita basis despite significant absolute emissions. The metric does not distinguish between emissions generated by export industries versus domestic consumption. Year-to-year fluctuations can occur due to economic cycles rather than structural changes.
